# Waterloo Through Temporal Combined Arms

**Date:** 2026-09-14
**Status:** historical counterfactual / systems-theory exercise

## Question

How would the Battle of Waterloo change if commanders explicitly optimized not only for capability complementarity, but for authority choreography, temporal persistence, and recomposability?

Working sequence:

```text
GUIDE -> RELEASE -> DISTRIBUTE -> PERSIST -> RECOMPOSE
```

This is not a claim that Napoleonic commanders lacked combined-arms thinking. It is a deliberate reinterpretation of the battle through a newer systems lens.

## The battle already exposes the problem

Waterloo can be read as a contest over who preserved useful options longest.

Wellington's system often behaved like a high-recomposability defense:

- infantry preserved behind the reverse slope rather than continuously exposed;
- strongpoints such as Hougoumont and La Haye Sainte functioned as distributed local anchors;
- squares gave infantry a robust defensive floor against cavalry;
- reserves could be committed selectively;
- local units could continue holding without every action requiring central retasking;
- Prussian arrival added an entirely new axis without requiring Wellington to abandon his existing defense.

By contrast, several French decisions converted options into hard-to-recover commitments:

- Hougoumont absorbed escalating effort without becoming the decisive breach;
- d'Erlon's attack committed a large infantry mass before the whole system had created an irreversible advantage;
- Ney's repeated cavalry commitment consumed mobile combat power against intact squares without the infantry/artillery combination needed to convert the pressure;
- the Imperial Guard remained the final central reserve, but by the time it was committed the French system had fewer intact options around it;
- Grouchy's detached force represents an operational commitment with very low recall bandwidth once distance and communications made later retasking difficult.

## Temporal-sovereignty interpretation

A core principle derived from Arcane Manifold is:

> New intention should govern new consequences. Old commitments should not be dragged sideways by every new observation unless deliberately reassigned.

At Waterloo, the opposite failure can be seen whenever the newest local impression becomes the new global priority.

The clearest example is cavalry commitment after the Allied line appeared to be withdrawing. If a fleeting observation immediately causes major mobile reserves to inherit a new mission, the present overwrites the larger plan.

A temporal-combined-arms system would ask first:

1. Is the observation reliable enough to justify a phase change?
2. Which forces should inherit the new intent?
3. Which existing commitments should preserve their old intent?
4. What remains available for recomposition if the interpretation is wrong?

## What Napoleon's side would change

If only the French side adopted the framework, the largest improvements would probably be organizational rather than technological.

### 1. Hougoumont becomes a shaping commitment, not an appetite

The objective would be to fix Allied forces and attention around the strongpoint while preserving the main French offensive mass elsewhere. Local autonomy would allow the fight to continue without repeated escalation from the center unless the strongpoint became genuinely exploitable.

The rule would be:

```text
SHAPE -> FIX -> STOP FEEDING IT UNLESS THE GEOMETRY CHANGES
```

### 2. The grand battery preserves intent by phase

Rather than allowing fires to chase whatever becomes visually urgent, artillery tasks would remain attached to phase objectives long enough to create cumulative effects. New batteries or newly freed guns could receive new intent without continuously rewriting every existing fire mission.

The principle is the same as the projectile mechanic that inspired the theory:

```text
NEW AIM OWNS NEW FIRES
OLD FIRES KEEP OLD PURPOSE
```

### 3. Cavalry becomes an exploitation arm, not a substitute for proof

Ney's cavalry would not be allowed to convert an ambiguous visual cue into repeated mass commitment without confirmation and support.

The cavalry's value lies partly in being an option that still exists.

A temporal-combined-arms commander would preserve a safe floor:

```text
INFANTRY / ARTILLERY CREATE THE BREACH
CAVALRY EXPLOITS IT
```

rather than gambling the mobile reserve on creating the breach by itself.

### 4. d'Erlon's attack is released with clearer autonomy and recomposition rules

Once committed, subordinate formations should know what conditions permit continuation, pause, lateral adjustment, or disengagement. Central control sets the mission and boundaries; local command manages the immediate geometry.

This reduces the need for the center to micromanage the attack while preserving a known path back into coherent reserve structure.

### 5. Grouchy's detachment gets conditional intent before communications fail

The most interesting operational application is that poor communications make future recall expensive.

Therefore the release order should carry more conditional logic at the moment of release:

```text
PURSUE PRUSSIANS
BUT IF CONTACT BECOMES UNCERTAIN,
OR THE MAIN BATTLE BECOMES DECISIVE,
REORIENT TOWARD THE MAIN ARMY / SOUND OF THE GUNS
```

The exact historical feasibility remains debatable, but the systems lesson is clean: when recall bandwidth will later be poor, encode more adaptive authority into the original order.

### 6. The Imperial Guard remains a recomposition lever longer

The Guard's value is not just combat power. It is the commander's final ability to change the shape of the battle.

Temporal Combined Arms treats reserves as stored future options, not idle power.

Committing them should therefore answer:

> What new system will exist after this commitment?

If the answer is merely 'one more frontal attack,' the reserve has been converted into force without buying enough new geometry.

## What Wellington and Blücher reveal

The awkward result is that the Allies already exhibit much of this theory.

Wellington's reverse-slope defense preserves combat power and uncertainty. Local strongpoints persist. Infantry squares provide a safe defensive floor. Reserves remain uncommitted until needed. Prussian convergence adds a fresh arm to an already functioning system rather than replacing it.

Thus Temporal Combined Arms may not overturn Waterloo so much as explain why the Allied system proved more durable:

```text
WELLINGTON PRESERVED OPTIONS
BLUCHER ADDED OPTIONS
FRENCH ATTACKS CONSUMED OPTIONS
```

## Counterfactual result

If the French alone used the framework well, the battle becomes more dangerous for Wellington.

The likely visible differences are:

- fewer French troops consumed at Hougoumont;
- fewer unsupported cavalry attacks;
- more deliberate synchronization of infantry, artillery, and cavalry;
- more mobile reserve power surviving into the late afternoon;
- a better chance of exploiting the fall of La Haye Sainte before Prussian pressure becomes overwhelming;
- greater operational flexibility in the use of Grouchy's force if conditional authority had been encoded early enough.

This does **not** produce an automatic French victory. Weather, terrain, timing, communications, fatigue, Allied discipline, Prussian arrival, and ordinary battlefield uncertainty remain enormous constraints.

If both coalitions adopted the framework equally well, the advantage may remain with the Allies because Wellington and Blücher already possessed a geographically distributed system whose parts could persist and converge without continuous central control.

## Distilled rule

Waterloo suggests a compact extension to the theory:

> **The best reserve is not merely unused combat power. It is preserved authority over the future shape of the battle.**

And the failure mode:

> **A commander loses twice when a local opportunity consumes both the force committed to it and the option that force represented.**
